Know the warning signs

Not Sure What Your Tree Needs?

Some problems are easy to spot. Others need a closer look. Dead branches, new trunk cracks, sudden leaning, or visible root movement can signal structural concerns. A qualified professional can help decide whether pruning, cabling, monitoring, or removal is appropriate.

Dead or dying trees

Bare branches in summer, peeling bark, fungus at the base, brittle twigs, and thinning crowns can indicate decline. Dead trees can drop limbs or fall without warning, especially after saturated soil or high wind.

Branches over a roof

Overhanging limbs damage shingles, clog gutters, and give animals a bridge to the attic. Heavy or dead limbs above living spaces deserve attention before the next storm.

Trees leaning after storms

A tree that has suddenly shifted, with soil cracking or lifting on one side, may have root damage. A slow, long-standing lean is different from a fresh one.

Cracked or damaged trunks

Deep vertical splits, hollow sections, large wounds, and V-shaped forks with included bark can weaken a tree. Cabling or removal may be appropriate depending on the defect.

Overgrown trees and bushes

Crowded growth blocks light, hides windows and doors, rubs siding, and can create safety concerns near driveways. Routine trimming restores clearance and improves appearance.

Stumps blocking yard use

Leftover stumps limit mowing, play space, and new landscaping. Grinding or removing them finishes a half-done project and helps prevent regrowth and pests.

Safety first: never approach downed electrical lines or attempt hazardous tree work yourself. Treat every fallen line as live, stay far back, keep children and pets away, and contact your electric utility or 911 if there is immediate danger.

Severe weather can leave trees leaning, branches suspended, or fallen trunks blocking access. These situations are hazardous, especially when electrical lines or damaged structures are involved.

What to do if a tree has fallen

  1. Get everyone to safety. Stay clear of the tree, especially if it is touching a roof, vehicle, or wire.
  2. Assume wires are live. Never touch a tree in contact with a power line. Call 911 for immediate danger and your utility for line hazards.

  4. Call us. Tell us the tree size, where it fell, and whether it is blocking access or resting on a structure.
  5. Protect your property. If safe, cover roof openings with a tarp. Do not climb onto a damaged roof.

Local knowledge

Tree Care Considerations for Georgia Properties

Georgia’s trees live with hot humid summers, heavy thunderstorms, occasional severe weather, and red clay soils that can drain poorly. Understanding those conditions helps you make better decisions.

Storm exposure

Spring storms, tropical remnants, and winter ice can break limbs or topple weakened trees. Saturated soil reduces root anchorage. Removing dead wood and addressing weak unions before storm season reduces risk.

Mature pines and hardwoods

Tall loblolly pines are common and can be vulnerable to beetles, drought stress, and wind snap. Mature oaks, maples, sweetgums, and tulip poplars may develop internal decay and heavy limbs not obvious from the ground.

Drainage and soil compaction

Construction and heavy equipment compress soil around roots. Georgia clay already holds water, so compacted yards can leave roots short of oxygen. Mulching and avoiding grade changes under the canopy help protect root health.

Seasonal pruning

Many trees are best pruned while dormant (late fall through winter), when structure is easy to see. Dead, broken, or hazardous limbs can be removed any time. Timing depends on species and goals.

Tree condition drives the decision

A healthy tree with a single defect may be a candidate for pruning or cabling; a tree with extensive basal decay may not. We explain why we recommend a particular option rather than defaulting to removal.

Choosing new trees wisely

Well-adapted species such as willow oak, red maple, American hornbeam, and river birch can thrive when matched to the site. Plant away from foundations and lines, and plan for full mature size.

Before you cut

Tree Removal Permits, HOAs, and Local Rules

Georgia does not have one statewide permit for homeowners who remove a tree from their own property, but many cities and counties adopt their own tree protection ordinances. Some rules apply to large specimen trees, heritage trees, buffer zones, or trees on development sites.

HOAs may also require approval before a tree is removed, particularly in front yards and common areas. Historic districts and conservation easements can add further restrictions.

Contact your city or county planning department and your HOA before scheduling removal. We can describe the work, provide photos, and help supply information the local office requests. Permit requirements are the property owner’s responsibility; we do not provide legal advice.

How to Choose a Tree Service Company in Georgia

After storms, unlicensed crews often knock on doors offering fast, cheap work. Use this checklist with any company you consider — including us.

Ask for proof of insurance

Request a current certificate of insurance and confirm it if you are unsure. An injury or roof damage from an uninsured crew can leave the property owner with the bill.

Get the scope in writing

The estimate should name the trees, the work, and the cleanup. “Cleanup included” is not enough — ask whether that means brush, logs, chips, and stump work.

Be careful with “topping”

Cutting large limbs back to stubs harms most trees and creates weak regrowth. A reputable company explains proper pruning cuts and alternatives.

Avoid pressure and large upfront payments

Be cautious with door-to-door offers, no-name trucks, and requests for large cash payments before work starts. A legitimate company has a verifiable address, phone, and online presence.

Check the business footprint

Look for consistent contact details across the website, Google Business Profile, and official social pages. Ours are linked in the footer.

Match the solution to the tree

A good tree professional explains alternatives — pruning, cabling, or monitoring — before recommending removal.

What is the difference between stump grinding and stump removal?

Stump grinding grinds the stump below the surrounding surface and leaves wood chips. Stump removal takes out more of the stump material, which may include roots, depending on the site and what you plan to do with the space. Learn more about stump grinding and stump removal.

How do I know if my tree is dangerous?

Warning signs include dead or hanging branches, a sudden lean, new trunk cracks, mushrooms or decay at the base, lifted soil around the roots, and branches over a roof. A qualified professional can assess the tree in person.

What is tree cabling?

Tree cabling installs support hardware in the canopy to limit movement between weak or split stems on suitable trees. It is an option when a tree has structural weakness but is otherwise worth preserving. See tree cabling.
