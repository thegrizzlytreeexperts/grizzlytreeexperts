What it is

Protect your home before the storm arrives

Storm prep support systems are a set of preventive treatments that reduce the odds of a limb or trunk failing in high wind, heavy rain or ice. After a careful inspection, our arborists combine the right methods for each tree: some need a few cables, others need lighter crowns, and a few need to be replaced.

It costs far less to fix a weak tree than to repair a roof, fence or vehicle. Pair this service with a tree health inspection, and if a tree is already down, we also handle emergency tree removal around the clock.

Hazard assessment

We check the trunk, root flare, unions and canopy for cracks, decay, lean and overextended limbs, then rank the risk to your home.

Cabling & bracing

Steel or dynamic cables and threaded rods support weak forks and heavy limbs. See our full tree cabling and bracing service.

Structural pruning

Selective cuts reduce sail area and end weight so wind passes through the crown. Learn more about tree trimming and pruning.

Deadwood & crown reduction

Dead, hanging and rubbing branches are removed and overweight limbs shortened, cutting the debris that becomes projectiles.

Root & soil care

Compacted or saturated soil weakens anchorage. We use air spade root care and fertilization to strengthen roots.

Lightning & guying options

For high-value or newly planted specimens we can recommend lightning protection and temporary guying where they make sense.

Georgia storm risks

Built for the way storms hit Georgia

From spring tornado warnings to tropical remnants and winter ice, Georgia trees face several kinds of stress. Local conditions make some trees more likely to fail, and we plan around each one.

WoodstockCantonRoswellAlpharettaMariettaKennesawAcworthAtlantaSavannah
  • Saturated red clay. After days of rain, clay holds water and loosens root grip, so even healthy oaks and poplars can tip in modest winds.
  • Tornado and straight-line wind season. March to May thunderstorms across Cherokee, Cobb, Fulton and Forsyth counties bring gusts that snap weak limbs.
  • Tropical remnants. Hurricane season begins in June, and remnants can drop heavy rain and wind hundreds of miles inland, as coastal areas like Savannah know well.
  • Weak-wooded species. Tall loblolly pines, Bradford pears, sweetgums and water oaks commonly split at forks or shed heavy limbs.
  • Winter ice. Ice loading can bring down overextended branches and power lines even after a light storm.
  • Dense neighborhoods and HOAs. Atlanta's canopy sits close to homes, cars and utility lines, so one failure can affect several properties.

Warning signs

Is your tree a storm hazard?

Call for a free assessment if you notice any of these around your property:

V-shaped double trunks

Tight forks with included bark are the most common failure point in storms.

Cracks, cavities, fungus

Mushrooms at the base or open wounds can point to internal decay.

Limbs over your roof

Heavy branches above a house, driveway or play area are worth checking before the next front.

Leaning or lifted roots

A new lean, soil heaving or exposed roots suggest anchorage problems and call for prompt attention.

Already have a tree that is failing? Ask about emergency tree removal or follow up with stump grinding once the tree is down.
