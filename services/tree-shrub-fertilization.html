Signs your trees and shrubs need fertilizer

Nutrient stress rarely looks dramatic at first. Georgia homeowners usually notice these early signals:

Yellowing leaves

Pale or yellow foliage with green veins is common in azaleas, gardenias and oaks when iron or manganese is locked up in the soil.

Thin canopy

Sparse leaves, small leaf size and dieback at the tips point to a tree using up its stored energy.

Slow growth

Little new growth year over year, especially on young trees planted in new-construction soil.

Early fall color drop

Leaves that brown or drop weeks early in summer often signal drought and nutrient stress together.

Few flowers

Dogwoods, crape myrtles and hydrangeas that bloom lightly may be missing phosphorus or potassium.

Repeated pest or disease trouble

Stressed trees attract borers, scale and fungal problems. Balanced nutrition helps trees defend themselves.

Why Georgia soils make fertilization matter

Georgia's soil changes from the mountains to the coast, and each region challenges trees differently. We tailor every plan to where your property sits.

Red clay in metro Atlanta

Across Fulton, Cobb, Gwinnett and DeKalb, Piedmont clay is acidic, dense and low in organic matter. Roots struggle to breathe and take up nutrients, even when fertilizer is applied on top.

Sandy coastal soil

Around Savannah, Pooler and Port Wentworth, sandy soil drains fast and washes nutrients past the root zone. Live oaks, magnolias and palms need steady, measured feeding.

Compacted new-build lots

Builders often strip topsoil and compact what remains. Trees in growing suburbs such as Woodstock, Canton, Cumming and Peachtree City frequently start life in poor soil.

Limestone and higher pH

In parts of northwest Georgia, including Cartersville, Rockmart and Cedartown, alkaline soil can cause iron deficiency in acid-loving shrubs.

Heat, drought and storms

Hot summers and dry spells stress roots. Trees also spend energy recovering from Georgia's storm seasons, so healthy reserves matter.

Mulch and leaf removal

Manicured lawns remove the leaf litter that feeds forest soil. Fertilization helps replace nutrients trees would get naturally in the woods.

Best timing in Georgia

Late winter to early spring: feed before new growth so roots and leaves have nutrients ready.

Mid-fall: trees store energy for winter and prepare for the next spring flush.

Summer: we avoid heavy nitrogen during heat waves and drought. Watering comes first, because fertilizing a dry tree can burn roots.

Tree and shrub fertilization questions in Georgia

When is the best time to fertilize trees in Georgia?

Late winter to early spring and mid-fall are the best windows. Feeding before spring growth supports new roots and leaves, and fall feeding helps trees store energy. We avoid heavy nitrogen in peak summer heat and during drought.

Does Georgia red clay need fertilizer?

Often yes. Piedmont clay is acidic, compacted and low in organic matter, so nutrients can be present but hard for roots to reach. A soil test tells us what is actually missing before we apply anything.

Can you over-fertilize a tree?

Yes. Too much nitrogen can burn roots, cause weak leafy growth and attract pests. That is why we base each plan on soil and tree condition instead of a fixed schedule.

How often should trees and shrubs be fertilized?

Most landscape trees benefit from feeding every one to three years, while shrubs such as azaleas and hydrangeas may need yearly attention. Frequency depends on species, soil and tree health.

How long until I see results?

Many trees show better leaf color and growth the next growing season. Slow-growing or stressed trees can take longer, since roots must recover first.

Can fertilization save a declining tree?

It can help when the cause is a nutrient shortage. If the decline comes from root damage, disease or structural problems, we recommend a tree health inspection first so we treat the real cause.
